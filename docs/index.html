What to watch next

A two-stage recommender trained on 1,000,209 MovieLens ratings. Five quick models shortlist about 187 movies for each person, then a gradient-boosted model reranks them. On ratings made after the training cutoff, its top 10 found 6.6% of the movies people went on to rate, against 3.9% for a most-popular list.

        How well does it work?

        Every model learned only from ratings made before 29 December 2000. Then it had to predict what 1,180 returning users rated after that, about 81 movies each. Recall@10 is the share of those movies that made a person's top 10. NDCG@10 also gives more credit for ranking them near the top. The two-stage model's recall is 1.7× the baseline's, and the best single model, EASE, reaches 4.9% on its own.

        How it works

        MovieLens 1M: 1,000,209 ratings (1 to 5 stars) from 6,040 people on 3,706 movies, April 2000 to February 2003.

        1. Split by time

          Ratings before 2 December 2000 are history. The reranker learns from what people rated between 2 December 2000 and 29 December 2000, and everything after 29 December 2000 is the test. A random split would let the models peek at the future.

        2. Shortlist with five models

          Most popular overall, most popular in the last 30 days, item-to-item similarity, matrix factorisation (SVD) and EASE each propose their top 50 to 100 unseen movies. Together that's about 187 per person, holding 38.8% of the movies they'll rate next.

        3. Rerank the shortlist

          A gradient-boosted classifier scores each candidate from 22 signals: the models' scores and ranks, the movie's popularity, trend, age and average rating, how mainstream the person's taste is, how well the genres match, and how popular it is with people of the same age and gender. The top 10 are the recommendations.

        4. Start new users somewhere sensible

          Someone with no ratings gets what has been popular lately with their age group and gender, mixed with overall popularity, until they've rated a few movies.

        Data: MovieLens 1M from GroupLens Research at the University of Minnesota. F. Maxwell Harper and Joseph A. Konstan, “The MovieLens Datasets: History and Context”, ACM Transactions on Interactive Intelligent Systems 5(4), 2015. This project isn't endorsed by GroupLens.
